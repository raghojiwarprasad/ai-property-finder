# Use-case journeys and acceptance scenarios

All people, organisations and work histories below are fictional design fixtures. They are not claims about real users, observed career transitions or measured learning results. Each journey specifies a learning outcome, not a career promise. Example schedules are sequences of sessions; actual pacing depends on evidence and the learner's available time.

## UC01: operations professional learning to use AI

### Starting context and goal

Anita studied commerce, spent five years coordinating construction suppliers and then completed an MBA. Her CV explicitly mentions purchase orders, delivery delays and comparison of bids. It does not demonstrate coding or AI proficiency. Her goal is “Use AI confidently in operations work.”

The system may use supplier comparison as an example. It must not infer low ability from her degree, invent a project she led, assume the MBA taught AI, or redirect her to a career change. Her goal is practical use, so initial depth emphasises task design, source checking and workflow control.

### First session

On goal confirmation, show “Your first milestone: create an AI task you can check and reuse.” The first scene presents a fictional comparison of supplier quotes. One answer includes an unsupported delivery guarantee. Anita identifies what evidence is missing, then rewrites a task brief to require source-backed claims and an explicit unknown state.

If she spots the unsupported claim immediately, shorten the introductory explanation and move into source boundaries. If she accepts the invented guarantee, show the supplied quote and ask which sentence supports it. If she asks for a simpler explanation, use a familiar distinction between a reported supplier promise and an assumption.

### Sessions and proof

Session 1: reliable versus merely fluent output; mark an unsupported claim. Session 2: task, context, constraints and output; write a reusable supplier-comparison brief. Session 3: retrieval; inspect what information the assistant was given. Session 4: human control; configure an approval threshold in a simulation. Session 5: evaluation; compare two outputs against a small rubric. Session 6: transfer; apply the same checks to a travel or customer-support scenario.

The milestone output is a source-constrained task brief and a reviewed comparison, with an explanation of when a human must decide. Demonstration requires an independent new scenario, not just a repaired version of the original supplier example.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

Given her confirmed supplier experience, the first example uses a relevant action such as bid comparison and identifies invented details as hypothetical. Given her incorrect answer, the system repairs evidence checking without labelling her generally weak. Given a new research goal later, these foundation demonstrations carry over and new technical prerequisites appear. No employer, college or country determines her maximum depth.

## UC02: CEO learning to lead AI decisions

### Starting context and goal

Daniel leads a mid-sized services business and has experience approving budgets and managing service quality. His goal is “Understand AI well enough to choose good investments and challenge vendors.” His CV suggests decision contexts; it does not prove knowledge of model limitations, evaluation or data architecture.

The first outcome is a defensible pilot decision, not the ability to train a model. The product still teaches underlying AI concepts when needed to judge claims. It does not reduce leadership learning to motivational summaries or outsource every technical question to a vendor.

### First session

Show two fictional proposals: an assistant drafting internal summaries with human review and an autonomous system approving customer refunds. Ask Daniel to identify the evidence and controls required before a pilot. The concept is that task risk, data availability and evaluation design matter alongside model capability.

If he focuses only on projected savings, introduce an example where review effort and exception handling remove the apparent benefit. If he already considers quality and failure conditions, deepen the exercise into evaluation-set design or buy-versus-build assumptions. Any financial numbers are labelled illustrative and are not market forecasts or investment advice.

### Sessions and proof

Session 1: match AI capabilities to tasks. Session 2: understand generation, retrieval and integration boundaries. Session 3: compare a general tool with a domain-specific workflow. Session 4: define quality, failure cost and human control. Session 5: design a small pilot with a baseline and success criteria. Session 6: critique a new vendor proposal without the earlier checklist.

The milestone output is a one-page pilot memo containing the task, baseline, data requirements, success measures, failure conditions, human owner and a proceed/pause decision. The rubric rewards justified trade-offs and explicit unknowns. It does not award credit for choosing the product's preferred vendor or agreeing that AI should always be adopted.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

Given strong business experience and weak AI evidence, the product preserves decision sophistication while teaching the missing concept simply. Given a request for architecture detail, it offers a deeper branch. Given a completed memo written mostly by the tutor, it records assistance and asks Daniel to defend a changed scenario independently.

## UC03: marketer learning to apply AI

### Starting context and goal

Maya has managed email campaigns, audience research and brand approvals. Her goal is “Use AI to create better campaigns while keeping claims and brand quality under control.” Her context supports briefs, segmentation and testing; it does not establish data-science expertise.

The first milestone is a sourced campaign brief and evaluation plan. The product should teach AI judgement and method through marketing work rather than become a generic copy generator. The learner must make decisions, inspect evidence and explain trade-offs.

### First session

Provide a fictional product factsheet and two AI-written claims. One claim upgrades “tested in a small internal trial” to “clinically proven for everyone”. Maya identifies the overstatement and rewrites the task instructions to prevent unsupported claims. The tutor connects AI output checking to the brand-approval process she already knows.

If she checks tone but misses factual overstatement, separate brand style from evidential support. If she catches both, move to conflicting audience evidence or designing a comparison between prompts. Avoid assuming persuasive writing skill means sound causal measurement.

### Sessions and proof

Session 1: recognise unsupported claims. Session 2: build a source-backed campaign brief. Session 3: generate useful variation within constraints. Session 4: compare outputs using a rubric. Session 5: design an A/B test while identifying what it can and cannot establish. Session 6: transfer the method to a different product with ambiguous evidence.

The output contains a brief, approved factual claims, two creative variants, an evaluation rubric and a measurement plan. The platform evaluates the reasoning and work against supplied evidence. It does not claim that a simulated campaign generated real conversions or revenue.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

Given a claim not present in the supplied material, feedback identifies the unsupported span. Given a claim supported by an alternative relevant passage, the rubric accepts it instead of requiring identical wording. Given “Go deeper”, the learner can study evaluation uncertainty or retrieval mechanics without being pushed into a new primary track.

## UC04: software developer learning applied AI

### Starting context and goal

Ravi builds web services using Python and HTTP APIs. He wants to “Build a reliable AI assistant that answers questions from documents.” His experience makes implementation examples useful, but the product must check rather than assume understanding of retrieval, evaluation, stochastic behaviour and tool permissions.

The first milestone is a small grounded-assistant evaluation in the practice environment. Code execution is a later gated environment capability unless isolation has been implemented. The early milestone can still teach retrieval and evaluations through supplied traces, configuration and controlled simulations.

### First session

Show a short request/response example and retrieved document snippets. The answer is plausible but cites an irrelevant paragraph. Ask Ravi to identify whether the failure arose from retrieval, answer synthesis, or insufficient information. A short prerequisite check verifies basic request/response understanding only if necessary.

If he knows APIs but confuses successful HTTP status with a correct answer, teach semantic evaluation directly. If he diagnoses the failure correctly, skip the generic “What is AI?” lesson and move into test-set design. If code syntax is the issue, repair that local prerequisite without discarding his goal.

### Sessions and proof

Session 1: model outputs and failure modes. Session 2: context and retrieval quality. Session 3: structured outputs and validation. Session 4: build an evaluation set with normal, missing-source and conflicting-source cases. Session 5: configure tool boundaries and fallback behaviour. Session 6: diagnose a new trace and justify an improvement using the same fixed tests.

The output is a configuration or later a small implementation, a versioned test set, evaluation results and a failure analysis. The rubric checks correct source use, explicit unknowns, handling of invalid outputs and permission boundaries. It does not equate a working chat window with a reliable AI application.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

Given demonstrated coding foundations, the planner skips redundant coding lessons but retains unknown AI concepts. Given a new provider or API detail, the lesson uses dated reviewed documentation or avoids vendor-specific claims. Given a failed tool-boundary test, the system teaches permissions and requires a fresh scenario before crediting the capability.

## UC05: nontechnical learner working towards AI research

### Starting context and goal

Leena has a humanities degree and operations experience, with no confirmed programming or advanced mathematics. Her goal is “Understand AI deeply and eventually do research.” The product must preserve that goal. It should explain the foundation route honestly and begin with an achievable scientific task.

The first milestone is understanding a simple prediction experiment. The longer path shows programming, mathematics, machine learning, neural networks, experiment design and paper reproduction. Only published milestones are actionable; the rest are visibly planned. The product does not promise research readiness within a fixed period.

### First session

Use a fictional dataset of delivery estimates and actual arrival times. Introduce the idea of predicting an outcome from past examples, then ask why testing on examples already used to tune the system can be misleading. Connect this to judging a process on new situations rather than rehearsed cases.

If the learner struggles with averages, provide a small numerical activity before asking about prediction error. If they explain leakage correctly, move forward even though the CV contains no technical qualification. Introduce a formal term only after the intuition is anchored, then make the formal definition explicit.

### Sessions and proof

Session 1: prediction, baseline and a fresh test. Session 2: data tables and simple arithmetic summaries. Session 3: functions and the idea of an input-output mapping. Session 4: introductory code or a code-reading activity, depending on evidence and environment availability. Session 5: training versus evaluation and leakage. Session 6: explain the result of a small supplied experiment and identify what it does not establish.

Later milestones add Python fluency, vectors, probability, calculus foundations, optimisation and learning theory as needed. A paper-reading task must check actual comprehension of methods, assumptions and limitations. Summarising an abstract with AI is not equivalent to understanding a paper.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

Given an ambitious goal and missing prerequisites, the path includes foundations without downgrading the destination. Given correct work on a prerequisite check, the learner advances regardless of formal education. Given unavailable advanced content, the interface states the limitation and offers available foundations rather than generating an unreviewed research curriculum as finished content.

## UC06: experienced ML practitioner with a narrow gap

### Starting context and goal

Omar has trained predictive models and wants to understand evaluation of tool-using language-model systems. He does not need an introductory course on regression. His goal is narrow, and the product should respect it without assuming that all adjacent agent concepts are already known.

### First session and adaptation

A quick diagnostic presents a trace with a successful final answer but an unauthorised intermediate tool action. Ask which evaluation signals matter. If he catches the action-policy failure, move to multi-step evaluation design. If he scores only the final answer, teach why process constraints and outcome quality require separate checks.

The system can show formal definitions, trace-level examples and test design directly. He can request intuition if needed. The first milestone output is an evaluation specification with failure classes, observable signals and representative cases. An expert-authored task bank must exist before this specialist milestone is sold as available; otherwise the product offers a preview and explicitly states the coverage gap.

### Acceptance and failure boundaries

The product does not force foundational lessons because every route begins with the same chapter. It does not flatter him into unearned mastery based on his title. A narrow goal stays narrow unless he elects to expand it. Any expert review requirement is shown honestly rather than presented as an instantaneous model judgement.

## UC07: learner without a CV

A student, career break returner or informal learner may have little formal experience. “Tell us instead” asks for a familiar activity, subject or project. A learner who writes “I help organise a community event and use spreadsheets” can receive examples about scheduling or comparing information. If they offer no useful context, the product starts with an ordinary general example and learns preferences later.

Acceptance: skipping the CV does not create a lower-quality account tier or block ambitious goals. The same goal and evidence logic applies. The system never fabricates an employment history to fill missing fields.

## UC08: returning, struggling or changing goals

A learner returns after three weeks. Their earlier demonstration is retained, and the product offers a short retrieval task. A wrong answer triggers focused support, not deletion of all progress. A right answer leads back into the milestone with minimal repetition.

A learner repeatedly struggles with one concept. The product changes representation, task size or prerequisite support and gives them control over pace. It avoids inferring laziness, intelligence or disability from activity logs.

A learner switches from marketing to building. Evidence for source checking and task framing carries over; code and implementation prerequisites become unknown until checked. The path-change preview explains both. Original work stays available under its original goal version.

## UC09: privacy-conscious learner and incorrect context

A learner removes an employer name and asks for generic examples. Future lessons use a permitted domain or neutral scenario. Existing shared snapshots that included the removed information are flagged for review and can be revoked; deleting private context must not be represented as automatically editing an already downloaded copy.

If an extracted date or project is wrong, the learner corrects it inline. The system updates the source relationship and invalidates dependent future examples. Learning evidence remains intact unless the correction changes the interpretation of the task itself.

## UC10: learner using accessibility support

A screen-reader user can upload a file, choose a goal, answer a task, inspect feedback and navigate the path without pointer input. A learner using enlarged text sees reflow rather than overlapping controls. A learner with limited time can shorten sessions without being labelled less capable. Diagrams have meaningful text alternatives; audio is optional and never the only route to content.

# Product vision: learn AI from where you are

## 1. The problem to solve

“I want to learn AI” hides several different needs. One person wants to judge vendor claims. Another wants better marketing work. Another wants to build reliable AI software. Another wants to understand and eventually contribute to research. A single beginner course, a chat window or a catalogue asks the learner to decide what matters before they understand the field.

The product's job is to turn an ambition into a useful sequence of learning and practice, then keep that sequence relevant as the learner changes. It should reduce the decisions required to begin while preserving the learner's control over the destination.

The central design tension is depth behind a simple experience. The system may reason over prerequisites, experience, examples, misconceptions and practice evidence. The learner should see a clear explanation, a meaningful task and the next useful step.

## 2. The promise and the unit of value

The promise is: “Learn AI in a way that starts with your experience and helps you achieve your goal.”

The smallest unit of value is a capability gained through an observable task. Examples include recognising when a confident answer lacks evidence, writing a testable AI task brief, identifying a failed retrieval example, choosing an evaluation metric, or explaining a train/test split. A watched video, an attractive path and a model-generated compliment are not equivalent units.

The product should provide value during the first visit even when the learner never returns. They should leave with one concept they can explain and one small output they can use. Continued use builds a coherent body of capability rather than a collection of isolated tips.

## 3. Who this serves

The full vision serves adults who want to use, lead, build or study AI. Current profession is context, not a track assignment. A marketer may want rigorous machine learning. A developer may want executive-level procurement knowledge. A person with an MBA may need programming foundations; another may already be proficient.

The first pilot should deliberately include contrasting backgrounds so personalisation can be tested. Acquisition can focus on one audience after evidence emerges, while the core data model remains goal-driven. Do not prematurely call the whole product “AI for software engineers” or “AI for executives”.

The buyer in the initial product is the learner. Teams can eventually purchase seats, but manager dashboards, organisation permissions and workforce assessment are outside this specification. They would introduce a second user and different incentives.

## 4. What personalisation actually means

Personalisation has six distinct dimensions. Context changes the situations used to teach. Destination changes required capabilities. Prior evidence changes the starting point. Performance changes support and sequencing. Learner feedback changes format and pacing. Time constraints change session size and scheduling.

These dimensions do not imply a fixed “learning style”. A person may benefit from a diagram for one concept, worked code for another and a verbal explanation for a third. The product responds to the task and learner feedback without labelling someone permanently as visual, analytical or nontechnical.

An experience profile should preserve meaningful relationships: role, project, action, tool, constraint and outcome. “Worked in construction” is weak context. “Coordinated suppliers, compared bids and tracked late deliveries” supports substantially better examples. The system must preserve the distinction between that explicit experience and an illustrative scenario invented to explain a concept.

## 5. The product personality

The tutor should be precise, warm and respectful. It acknowledges the learner's existing competence without exaggerating what the CV proves. It can say, “You already know how to compare supplier bids; let's use that structure to understand evaluation.” It should not say, “I understand your entire career” or “You are now an AI expert.”

Feedback names the exact move that worked or failed. “You defined an output format, but the source restriction is still missing” is useful. “Amazing work!” without evidence is not. When the learner is wrong, the system explains the misconception, offers a smaller step and gives another opportunity.

The interface should feel like a calm personal study workspace. Strong typography, generous reading space, purposeful diagrams and visible work matter more than dashboards, decorative animation, streak pressure or a futuristic robot mascot.

## 6. The durable learning loop

Understand the learner's context and goal. Select a meaningful next capability. Teach through a relevant example. Ask the learner to act. Evaluate the action with an explicit rubric. Update the evidence. Choose the next activity and explain why it changed.

The loop must include both support and independence. Providing a worked answer can be excellent teaching, but a later independent attempt is needed to know whether the learner can use it. The system records the level of assistance and uses it when making progression decisions.

Every several activities, the learner should encounter the same underlying concept in a new domain or a more complex task. This prevents personalisation from becoming dependence on one familiar analogy.

## 7. The experience should grow with the learner

Early on, the product does more planning and supplies more structure. As understanding grows, learners should be able to inspect assumptions, choose harder tasks, compare approaches, design evaluations and propose their own projects. “Personalised” should eventually mean greater agency, not permanent dependence on a tutor.

Research is the deepest version of this progression. It requires mathematical and programming fluency, experiment design, paper comprehension, reproduction and critique. A foundational explanation about neural networks is not a complete research pathway. Planned milestones must make that distance visible without discouraging ambition.

## 8. What may make this product defensible

The working hypothesis is that a coherent record of learning evidence, misconception repair, transfer and useful work can improve recommendations over time. A CV parser or a general chat model alone is easy to substitute. A well-designed curriculum, excellent tasks and trustworthy continuity are harder to deliver consistently.

This is a hypothesis about product quality, not a proven competitive moat. More clicks do not automatically produce better teaching. The team must measure which changes actually improve independent performance and retention, with appropriate consent for using learning data in product evaluation.

## 9. Experience principles that resolve trade-offs

- First useful activity before a complete long-term plan: generate the next milestone first and progressively disclose the rest.
- Enough evidence to adapt, enough autonomy to explore: users can preview or challenge content without a gate being misrepresented as certification.
- Personal relevance with intellectual accuracy: an analogy may simplify, but its limit must be explained when material to the concept.
- Foundation before unsupported acceleration: a demanding destination remains available even when the route must include additional preparation.
- Measured progress before decorative progress: show concrete achievements and evidence recency rather than a global completion percentage for “all AI”.
- Useful sharing after value: the learner chooses what to share, and the recipient receives a meaningful experience before an acquisition prompt.

## 10. What this vision does not promise

No guaranteed professional expertise, fixed time to research readiness, employment outcome, causal claim about career transitions or guaranteed viral growth. No automatic inference of thousands of accurate personal facts from a short CV. The product can be ambitious while explicitly representing what it knows, what it suspects and what still needs to be learned.
